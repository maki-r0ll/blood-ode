Some 500 Years Ago

She sat atop the dais, unmoving, her hands folded neatly in her lap. The silence in her chamber was thick, broken only by the wind pressing gently against the stained glass panes. She waited—she had been waiting for what felt like an eternity. Her gaze drifted to the window, where twilight stretched long shadows across the floor. The room around her gleamed with perfection. Not a cushion out of place, not a thread left undone. Silk curtains framed the tall windows, and candlelight flickered in golden sconces. Everything was beautiful, everything untouched—and yet, the ache in her chest made the beauty feel cold.

The hinges groaned as the door eased open. Her breath caught before she even saw him. He stepped through, and the air seemed to shift.

“My love…”
Her voice was barely more than breath, caught somewhere between disbelief and relief. Her lips parted, eyes wide, as if the very sight of him undid all the tension she had carried for years.

“Darling…”
His voice reached her with softness, but his body told another story. Blood clung to the edges of his armor, dull and crusted, and dust lined the folds of his cloak. His expression was weary, eyes rimmed with exhaustion—but they never left her.

She rose and crossed the room without hesitation, her arms already moving to embrace him. She pressed herself to his chest, unbothered by the grit and the blood. Her fingers curled against the plates of his armor, needing to feel that he was real, that he had returned.

“I’ve been waiting for you,” she said, her breath trembling. “You know how much I hate to wait.”
Her voice cracked as tears broke free, rolling down her cheeks one by one. Her words were uneven, fragmented under the weight of everything she had held inside.

“To keep you waiting is my greatest sin,” he murmured, his voice heavier now.
He pulled off one gauntlet and lifted his hand to her hair, threading his fingers through the silver strands with slow reverence.

She looked up at him, her smile fleeting, burdened by emotion. Her brow drew together as if holding back more than she could say.

“What news from Ingwar Lhinc?” she asked, her voice quiet, but strained. “Will the wars end?”
She stared at him, searching for something—hope, assurance, anything that might steady her. Her hands gripped his arms tightly, as though if she let go, the world would fall apart again.

He hesitated, his jaw tightening.

“I…” He brushed her cheek with his thumb, his gaze fixed on hers. “I bring grave news. Our sons…”
The words hung between them. He faltered, watching the fear take shape across her face. Her features twisted—not in surprise, but in confirmation of the dread she had long carried.

He kissed her then, gently, his lips resting on hers without urgency. When he pulled back, he rested his forehead against hers. A moment passed. Stillness. Grief beginning to stir behind their silence. And though it did not ease the pain, the closeness offered something solid to hold on to. Something that might carry them through what would come next.

He continued, voice fractured, unraveling with every word.

“Their fates have been sealed in the days to come. I tried—I could not stop it.”
The promise of safety he once clung to had turned to ash in his mouth. The futures they had whispered about, dreamed of—dust now, slipping through his fingers.

She let out a choked sob, her hands trembling. When she spoke, her voice was no longer soft. It burned.

“I will not be the one to carry my sons to their graves, Otto. I cannot.”

His face twisted, the weight of her words landing with brutal clarity. He couldn’t meet her eyes.

“Lilith… please. Not now. Not like this.”

Her gaze snapped to him, burning with rage. There was no softness left.

“You told me sending them away would keep them safe,” she hissed, every word like a blade. “That they would be protected. That we were doing what was right. But this—this is not safety. I might as well carve their names into stone myself.”

She stepped back, as if the air between them had curdled.

“How can you expect me to be calm? To accept this? Our sons walk toward death, and I can do nothing but watch. My only sons. Born from my body, given away for their survival—and now you tell me even that was not enough?”

Her hands clenched at her sides, her voice rising, laced with venom.

“Queen Elara’s promises are lies wrapped in silk. You fight her wars. You hand over our resources. You speak of loyalty, of duty. You say sending them across time will protect them—but here we stand, and I feel their deaths written already in the stars. Inked in blood. My blood.”

She turned from him, jaw tight, eyes burning with more than tears. A mother’s fury. A queen’s betrayal. A wound that would not heal.

“This is the way it has to be,” he said, but the words barely made it out. “We could not save them. Maybe… maybe their futures still shift, maybe something changes…” But his voice had already broken. He was breaking. He had fought, bled, begged the fates. And still, he had failed.

“I’ve done everything,” he whispered. “Everything in my power to kill the Dragon King. But there is nothing left. There is nothing more I can give.”

She looked at him—really looked at him. The man she had waited for. The man she had loved. She stepped forward with quiet purpose, her grief no longer trembling but sharpened into resolve. She brought both hands to his face, fingers threading gently through his long, tangled hair, now streaked with sweat and dust and time. Her voice was low, but steady.

“I am death. And only in death shall I serve any purpose.” She held his gaze, unwavering. “My purpose lies with them. With our sons. With you. If I must carry us all into eternity to keep us whole, I will. I will not go quietly. But I will go willingly—with open arms. And I will watch them. Even after you are gone. Even when the stars forget their names. I will give them every sliver of chance, every last breath I have left. I will tear through time itself, through death, through ruin, through the end of all things—until I bring them home.”

Some 200 Years Later

The castle was impossibly vast. Its ceilings stretched into shadow, swallowed by distance. Door after door lined the endless halls—tall, ornate, silent things—each one closed, each one leading somewhere long forgotten. The floor beneath her feet was cold marble veined with silver. Light trickled through stained glass windows that shimmered with colors no longer seen in the waking world.

Lilith walked barefoot through the corridor, her footsteps making no sound. Her sons stood beside her, or what remained of them. They were pale, flickering things now—shapes pulled from memory and sorrow. Ghosts, perhaps. Or something less. Yet their hands were warm in hers.

They had saved the world. They had bled for it, given their bodies, their names, their futures. Gods had crumbled beneath their resolve, and even as the heavens screamed and the stars burned out, they did not falter. They were brave. They were good. And now, they were gone. But she had found them. At last.

Doors stretched endlessly ahead, vanishing into a horizon that did not exist. There would be no fanfare. No throne. No choir to sing of their return. Only this long, quiet walk through eternity. Only her. Only them.

She had always been death. She had waited in silence. She had hunted across timelines. She had held the names of her sons close against her heart, even when time tried to steal them from her. And now she walked them home, one door at a time.

Home, she thought. I can finally bring them home.
